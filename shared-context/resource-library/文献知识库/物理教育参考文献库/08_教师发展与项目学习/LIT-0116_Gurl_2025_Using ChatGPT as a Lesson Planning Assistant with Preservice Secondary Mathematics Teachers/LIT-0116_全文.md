# Using ChatGPT as a Lesson Planning Assistant with Preservice Secondary Mathematics Teachers

Theresa J. Gurl<sup>1</sup> · Mara P. Markinson<sup>1</sup> · Alice F. Artzt<sup>1</sup>

Accepted: 1 October 2024 / Published online: 22 October 2024   
© The Author(s) 2024

## Abstract

The purpose of this qualitative study was to examine the use of ChatGPT as a lesson-planning assistant with preservice teachers (PSTs) of secondary mathematics. PSTs asked ChatGPT to solve a mathematics problem and to assist with lesson planning in a microteaching context in a methods course. The PSTs first developed parts of their microteaching lesson plan individually, then asked ChatGPT to do the same thing. The PSTs then reflected on the output generated by ChatGPT. They then used ChatGPT any way they saw fit throughout the planning process. An analysis of the PSTs’ reflective statements about ChatGPT’s output revealed the importance of critical and evaluative reflection. Although PSTs were generally accurate in their assessment of pedagogical output, noting that ChatGPT’s suggested lessons were teacher-centered and repetitive, indicating little knowledge of students’ needs; they were less accurate about their assessment of mathematical output, often attributing incorrect solutions as a “different approach” to the problem. The findings of this study have implications for the value of a reflective process for PSTs when using ChatGPT and other chatbots for lesson planning and solving mathematics problems, and suggest that a framework for critical examination of mathematical output may be necessary in the age of GAI.

Keywords Generative AI · ChatGPT · Secondary mathematics · Preservice teachers

## Introduction

Generative AI (GAI) chatbots such as ChatGPT are poised to fundamentally change the work of teachers, and therefore teacher preparation programs are at the forefront of integrating the use of GAI in educational settings (MacDowell & Korchinsk, 2023). GAI is poised to change how teachers develop assessments, plan lessons, create word problems, and influence other components of teaching. For example, Chat-GPT may be used to grade student work and create progress reports for students. It can also translate information to facilitate communication with non-English-speaking students and parents (Trust et  al., 2023), to determine the readability of text, to track and monitor student progress, and to provide feedback (Celik et al., 2022). Thus, it is essential that preservice teacher education programs incorporate the use of GAI chatbots, so that preservice teachers (PSTs) are prepared to use these technologies in productive and useful ways as they enter the teaching profession (Tate et al., 2023). Although GAI can be useful, it is imperfect, especially in mathematics (Wardat et al., 2023), and teachers, particularly PSTs, must be reflective about the output generated by chatbots such as ChatGPT. Tate et al. assert that GAI is “fallible but useful” (2023, p. 7).

One use of GAI is to generate lesson plans and to make suggestions regarding pedagogical approaches (Trust et  al., 2023). Another use of GAI is to prompt it to solve mathematics problems, so that PSTs are aware of different possible solutions to the problems, although GAI is sometimes incorrect, performing at the level of an average graduate student (Frieder et al., 2024). Thus, using ChatGPT’s output requires reflective analysis on the part of the user. Lesson planning is of particular interest, since researchers have found that PSTs have difficulty planning lessons (Cevikbas et al., 2024; Lim et al., 2018; Sahin-Taskin, 2017). Many PSTs first encounter the requirement to plan a lesson in a methods class, often through a microteaching experience. This study examines the incorporation of ChatGPT, specifically version GPT-3.5, into a lesson-planning experience in a methods course, where PSTs were instructed to use Chat-GPT as an assistant for writing a lesson plan for a microteaching experience referred to as a “mini-lesson”. In this study, PSTs interacted with ChatGPT to obtain ideas for lesson planning, and to examine solutions to the mathematics problem used to launch their mini-lesson. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to answer the following research questions: (1) How did PSTs react to the mathematical output produced by ChatGPT? (2) What did PSTs find useful/not useful when using ChatGPT to plan a mini-lesson?

First, we discuss theoretical frames for teacher reflection and attitudes toward the use of GAI, then the literature on PSTs’ issues with lesson planning, and the literature on microteaching, then discuss the use of GAI such as ChatGPT for mathematics, and for lesson planning. We then discuss the procedures of the study and data analysis. Finally, we present results and discuss implications for the field regarding the use of GAI in teacher preparation.

## Literature Review and Theoretical Framework

## Theoretical Framework

The theoretical frame for this study draws upon the work of Artzt (1999), Knoth et  al. (2024), and Celik et al. (2022). We used the Artzt framework because it is infused throughout the authors’ teaching program, is familiar to the PSTs, and holds reflective practice at the center of every aspect of instruction. This frame asserts that instructional practice (which mainly concerns tasks as in the present study, but also, learning environment, and discourse) is a byproduct of teachers’ knowledge, beliefs, and goals. These components drive teachers’ preactive thoughts (lesson planning), interactive thoughts (monitoring of lesson), and postactive thoughts (reflective ideas about improving the lesson).

The framework emphasizes the critical importance of teachers’ knowledge and beliefs about mathematics, pedagogy, and students. Further, the Artzt framework emphasizes the importance of student, rather than teacher-centered instruction. Although this may not be internationally accepted, student-centered instruction is emphasized as a goal in the USA (and in local districts) given the findings of the TIMSS studies and recommendations of the NCTM (NCTM, 2000; Gurl et al., 2016). The work of Knoth et  al. is a framework for AI literacy, both generic and domain-specific, the domain in the present study being education.

Similar to the Artzt frame, Knoth et al. include cognition (knowledge), behavior (skills), and attitudes (values) about AI and its capabilities and limitations. Celik et  al. found in their review of AI-related literature that AI-related advantages for teachers are related to planning, implementation, and assessment of lessons, while disadvantages are related to lack of reliability of AI output, limited access, and lack of technical knowledge. Although the Celik et al. study was not focused on generative AI, the findings are applicable to the use of GAI.

The main emphasis for this study is planning for components of the mathematical task, and examining the PSTs’ reflections related to the use of ChatGPT for planning. Components of tasks are modes of representation, motivation, difficulty level, and sequencing (Artzt, 1999; Artzt & Armour-Thomas, 1999; Artzt et  al., 2015). Modes of representation are “forms for representing mathematical concepts”, while motivation refers to tasks that “attract and sustain (learners’) attention and emotional investment” (Artzt et al., 2015, p. 14). Difficulty level and sequencing refers to the “complexity … and order in which the teacher makes the task salient for (students)” (Artzt et al., 2015, p. 15).

The main emphasis of this study is on the tasks, although other components of the framework also emerged at times as part of the PSTs’ reflective process. The work of Knoth et al. parallels that of Artzt since both emphasize the role of cognition/knowledge, along with beliefs/attitudes. Knoth et  al., however, emphasize the need for AI literacy, an essential facet of this study. Some of the findings of this study extend and in some cases mildly contradict those of Knoth et  al. The findings of Celik et  al. are framed around planning, implementation, and assessment, also parallel to the Artzt framework. These three studies provide a robust frame for examining PST interactions with ChatGPT.

## Literature on PST Lesson Planning and Microteaching

Lesson planning, which is “the thinking that teachers do as they prepare to teach lessons” (Artzt et  al., 2015, p. 25), is often quite difficult for PSTs (Cevikbas et al., 2024; Lilly et al., 2024; Lim et al., 2018; Sahin-Taskin, 2017). Planning a lesson should result in a document referred to a “lesson plan” which includes an outline of the important components of the lesson. In our program, this includes an aim, objectives, motivational questions, tasks and activities, and assessment. PSTs’ difficulties in lesson planning are often related to a lack of knowledge about students’ misconceptions and how students think about mathematical concepts and designing or applying real-life applications of what they were teaching into their lessons (Cevikbas et al., 2024). Even high-performing PSTs were much more skillful in enacting lessons but “have yet to develop skills in lesson planning” (Lim et  al., 2018, p. 528). Further, PSTs often find it difficult to create their own plans and find suitable activities and problems for students, although there is some evidence for the use of technology to facilitate lesson planning (Cevikbas et  al., 2024). The initial steps for planning are especially difficult for PSTs, particularly finding suitable activities, and these difficulties often persist for early-career teachers as well (Lilly et  al., 2024; Sahin-Taskin, 2017).

Given PSTs’ difficulties with lesson planning, they often benefit from a scaffolded approach to learning how to plan a lesson. Lim et  al. (2018) found that PSTs struggle with creating their own lessons, and performed better when modifying existing lesson plans or synthesizing components of lessons, rather than creating their own lessons from scratch. Lilly et al. (2024) found that earlycareer teachers improved their lesson planning when they were able to make explicit connections between the mathematics content they were teaching and knowledge of how students would react.

Another scaffold that many teacher preparation programs often incorporate is microteaching. Microteaching is a form of practice lesson planning and teaching to peers that often takes place in a methods course (Bell, 2007) and “provide(s) PSTs with more authentic opportunities to enhance their knowledge and skillset” (Jakopovic et  al., 2023, p. 29). Microteaching is often complementary to traditional field experiences and may contribute to a teacher’s improved sense of self-efficacy. Furthermore, microteaching includes opportunities for peer feedback and PST reflection (Arsal, 2014; Jakopovic et al., 2023).

The literature specific to the planning process as related to microteaching is less conclusive. Although microteaching always involves planning a lesson, the process by which this occurs is not a prevalent in research findings or discussion. While Bell (2007) simply mentions that the microteaching process allows PSTs to have some experience planning a lesson, Arsal (2014) and Jakopovic et al. (2023) say a bit more, including the benefit of PSTs setting gaining experience identifying learning objectives and standards for their plans. According to Arsal (2014), PSTs became more efficient in planning and developed selfconfidence in planning, while the PSTs in Jakopovic et al. (2023)’s study found the opportunity to practice writing lesson plans beneficial. Therefore, it is clear that any additional support for PSTs in writing lesson plans, and opportunities to practice writing plans, would be beneficial.

## Literature on GAI for Lesson Planning

There is a growing literature base related to using GAI such as ChatGPT in educational contexts. ChatGPT is a large language model which is trained on large datasets and is able to generate text (Marr, 2023). It can assist teachers in creating lesson plans, assessments, translating language for non-native speakers, creating instructional materials and can even function as a mentor for novice teachers (Lo, 2023). Celik et  al. (2022) found that AI can provide information about students’ backgrounds and make suggestions about appropriate content and activities. ChatGPT, however, is known to have varying levels of performance, particularly in mathematics (Frieder et al., 2024; Shryock, 2023), and may be getting worse in its ability to do mathematics correctly (Chen et al., 2023). However, there is also the chance it will improve. In the present study, the uneven quality of the GAI output provides ample opportunity for the PSTs to reflect critically.

An important drawback to using ChatGPT in planning mathematics lessons is that it often produces incorrect mathematical output (Wardat et al., 2023) and has been found to be limited in its ability to plan entire lessons (Gurl et al., in process). There have, however, been recent findings that there were better student outcomes when teachers used lessons produced by ChatGPT rather than lessons prepared by teachers alone (Karaman & Goksu, 2024). Kehoe (2023) found that the benefits of using ChatGPT for lesson planning included saving time, increasing creativity, and personalization of lessons but emphasized the need for quality control and awareness of the inherent bias in GAI algorithms. Thus, there remains much uncertainty about the usefulness of using ChatGPT to plan lessons and do mathematics at this time.

What is certain, however, is that PSTs need to be able to critically reflect on the correctness, usefulness, and overall value of output produced by GAI chatbots. Although GAI output can be incorrect, especially when doing mathematics, it is likely that PSTs (and others) will continue to use it, since, as Tate et al. (2023) state, “the proverbial cat is already out of the bag”, and even though there are weaknesses, there are also “powerful affordances” (p. 8). As stated in our theoretical frame, reflection is at the center of all aspects of teaching (Artzt, 1999; Artzt & Armour-Thomas, 1999; Artzt et al., 2015), and PSTs’ cognition, attitudes, and behavior (Knoth et al., 2024) contribute to their critical reflection about and evaluation of ChatGPT’s output in the context of their teaching.

## Procedures

## Subjects and Context

Subjects of this qualitative study were a convenience sample of PSTs (n = 22) who were enrolled in either of two sections of a secondary mathematics methods course at a public, urban, university in the northeastern US. Seventeen of the PSTs were undergraduate students, and five were graduate career changers. A method course requirement was for each PST to plan and teach a mini-lesson (i.e., microteaching) to the rest of the class, who role-played as secondary students, and to the professors, who are the second and third authors of this work. The PSTs were required to select their mini-lesson problem from problem-solving tasks from the class textbooks (Gurl et al., 2012, 2013) and then develop a lesson using the task as a motivation for the lesson. Although each PST did an individual mini-lesson, there were two sections of the course, so some topics were planned and taught by two different individuals. The lessons were taught twice, concurrently, but in two different classrooms, because there were two different sections of the course taught by two different professors (i.e., the second and third author of this work).

The professors and authors of this work emphasized a problem-solving approach to teaching as consistent with the standards of the National Council of Teachers of Mathematics (NCTM) in the US. The NCTM’s standards recommend using problems with students to “build new mathematical knowledge” (NCTM, 2000, p. 52). The preface of the required texts note that the tasks are recommended to be used as launch problems for teaching (Gurl et al., 2012, 2013).

## Data Collection and Analysis

In order to support the PSTs in planning their mini-lessons over the course of several weeks, data were collected in two phases with professor (and ChatGPT) support. Once each PST chose their mathematical task, the first phase included a homework assignment at the early planning stages, well before teaching the mini-lesson. The assignment was for each PST to complete their chosen mini-lesson mathematical task with no assistance, and to begin the lesson planning process by writing a focus question (referred to as an “aim” as consistent with requirements of local districts) and behavioral objectives for their lesson.

As part of the same homework assignment, they were then asked to engage with ChatGPT by doing the following and entering responses to these Phase 1 items into an online form: (1) Ask ChatGPT to do your mini-lesson task. How do your solutions compare? (2) Ask ChatGPT to formulate an Aim and objectives for a secondary mathematics lesson based on your mini-lesson task. Copy and paste the output. Feel free to modify your prompts as you see fit, but be sure to attach the entire output. (3) Re-write your Aim and objectives for your mini-lesson after using ChatGPT as an assistant. If you are not making any changes to your Aim or objectives, explain why. (4) Reflection: How did using ChatGPT affect your Aim and Objectives? Did you find consulting with ChatGPT to be similar to or different from working with a peer or a professor? Explain.

This initial interaction with ChatGPT took place during September 2023. Over the next few weeks while developing their lesson plans, PSTs were instructed to continue to use ChatGPT as a resource for the planning process in any way they saw fit, but there was no requirement that they use it any particular number of times. Over the semester, each PST taught their lesson to their class and professor, submitting pre- and post-lesson thoughts, lesson plans, and activities. Two or three mini-lessons took place per class meeting, and the class met once per week. After they taught, the PSTs responded to the following Phase 2 items as part of their post-lesson thoughts: Summarize your impression of your interactions with ChatGPT regarding your mini-lesson. State at least one example of what ChatGPT provided that was and was not useful. Please include screenshots of your exchange with ChatGPT and explain your reasoning. The phases of data collection are summarized in Table 1.

Data from this study were analyzed using qualitative coding methods as described by Charmaz (2006) and Saldaña (2016). According to Charmaz, “coding means naming segments of data with a label that simultaneously categorizes, summarizes and accounts for each piece of data” (2006, p. 43). Data were coded incident to incident; that is, each PST’s response to a data collection prompt was considered to be a single “incident” that was assigned a deductive code that was determined prior to analysis, although we also looked for new ideas that might emerge from the data. Saldaña recommends deductive coding when the “inquiry is theory-driven and targets specific experiences, phenomena, actions, and so on that you are certain will appear in the empirical materials” (2016, p. 39). For example, when coding the mathematical solutions, we knew we were looking for correctness; when examining the pedagogical output, we expected to see evidence of Artzt’s framework since that was heavily emphasized in the methods class.

The first author used incident to incident coding to determine correctness for each PST’s solution, followed by determining correctness of Chat-GPT’s associated mathematical output and then each PST’s reflection about Chat-GPT’s output for accuracy of evaluation. A solution was coded as “correct” if it was completely correct, or if the only errors were minor, such as arithmetic, and there were no conceptual errors. The first author then examined ChatGPT’s solution to the same problem, and coded ChatGPT’s solution for correctness using the same standard. Then, the PST’s solution and ChatGPT’s solution were each coded for correctness, and the PST’s reaction to ChatGPT’s solution was coded for whether they identified the correctness of ChatGPT’s output, and how they compared ChatGPT’s output. Although there were 22 PSTs in the study, three of them did not correctly follow instructions; therefore, those PST responses could not be included in the analysis.

## Results

This section will be organized by the results for each of the research questions, first reporting the PSTs’ reactions to the mathematical output and then by discussing what the PSTs found useful and not useful when using ChatGPT to plan a mini-lesson. For each, a discussion of the coded results will be followed by specific statements by the PSTs and output generated by ChatGPT to better illustrate the PSTs’ interactions with the chatbot as viewed through the lens of the reflective framework (Artzt, 1999; Artzt & Armour-Thomas, 1999; Artzt et al., 2015).

## PSTs’ Reactions to ChatGPT’s Mathematical Output

There were 19 PST and associated ChatGPT responses coded for the mathematical problems, which included algebra, geometry, data analysis, and percent, at the middle- and high-school level (US grades 7–12). Of these, 15 of the PSTs’ mathematical solutions were coded as “correct”, while only six of the ChatGPT responses were coded as “correct”. We then compared the code for the PST solution to the code for ChatGPT solution for the same problem. For the 19 PSTs’ responses, coding revealed five instances where the PST and ChatGPT solutions were both correct, ten where the PST was correct, but ChatGPT’s response was not, three where both were incorrect, and one where the PST was incorrect and ChatGPT was correct. These results are summarized in Table 2.

We then coded the PST reflections about the ChatGPT mathematical output. Among the five instances where both the PST and ChatGPT correctly solved the problem, the PST reactions said just that, mentioning that ChatGPT’s solutions were the same, but some noted that different mathematical approaches were used, along with different language and formatting. An example is the PST’s reflection about task 1.4. The task is reproduced in Table 3, followed by the PST’s response.

The PST solved the problem correctly, and stated the following in their reflection about ChatGPT’s correct solution to the same problem:

The solution to part b and c of my task were the same. But when it comes to part a and d, it took a different approach. For part a, instead of showing the difference in prices using the two different methods, it multiplied the discount and shows what the actual combined percent she gets off the original item. And, for part d, I state the commutative property of multiplication for the order of application not mattering, but the ai (sic) showed that using each method the same amount would be taken off the original item. (PST response)

This was an accurate assessment of the differences between responses, where both the PST and ChatGPT were correct. The PST applied their mathematical knowledge while reflecting on the output, noting differences in the approach to the problem. As stated above there were only five instances where both the PST and ChatGPT solved the problem correctly.

There were 10 instances where the PST solved the problem correctly while ChatGPT did not. In all but one of these 10 instances, the PSTs did not correctly identify the issues with ChatGPT’s response. First, we will share the one instance where the PST correctly evaluated and reflected upon ChatGPT’s incorrect output. We will then share three instances where the PST did not correctly identify the errors in ChatGPT output, two for the same task. For each instance, the task will be shared, then ChatGPT’s output with the incorrect solution, along with the PST’s reflection about and evaluation of the generated solution.

## PSTs’ Reactions to the Usefulness of ChatGPT’s Output

To determine what the PSTs found useful and not useful when planning, and answer research question 2, we coded responses to the Phase 1 and Phase 2 items related to pedagogy (see above for Phase 1 and 2 items). We first determined whether the PST stated that they made any changes to their initial attempts at lesson planning, then coded whether they thought working with ChatGPT was different or similar to working with a professor or peer. We then coded the responses to the Phase 2 reflective prompt. The responses to this item were coded for what was stated by the PSTs to be useful and not useful as either being related to content or pedagogy, and then what aspect of the theoretical framework were evident in terms of planning for tasks, learning environment, or discourse (Artzt et al., 2015).

A summary of coding of whether a change was made follows. Of the 22 coded responses, 20 indicated that they changed their aim or objectives in some way, while two did not make any changes. One of the two PSTs who did not make a change stated:

I am not making any changes to my Aim or my objectives. I found that the objectives ChatGPT listed were very similar to the ones I used in my own assignment. ChatGPT also included objectives that weren’t explicitly relevant to the task, such as “Analyzing real-world scenarios” relating to rational and irrational numbers. I also found that phrases such as “Use critical thinking skills” were not measurable and should not be included in objectives. (PST response)

A PST who did make a change stated, “ChatGPT gave me a better understanding of my goal in teaching my mini-lesson, and it affected my objective. I was struggling to come up with my objectives for my mini-lesson, however, ChatGPT was able to list them all out for me.” Another PST who made a change stated, “The results of ChatGPT helped me improve my aim and objectives. The results of ChatGPT were more comprehensive and thoughtful.” Another PST (who was writing a lesson based on a task related to earning more points versus performing better in basketball) went into more detail:

ChatGPT did not affect my Aim, but it greatly affected my Objectives. I liked the way that ChatGPT had more specific objectives that pertained to the lesson. The only aspect that I did not like was relating the entire lesson to the basketball percentages, so I changed the objectives to omit that aspect. I was able to alter the objectives in a way that every scenario could apply to the objectives, not just basketball. After asking ChatGPT to generalize the objectives, they became much more clear and could apply to all quantities of ratios rather than just basketball. This was very helpful because the objectives were separated into categories in a similar manner that is expected of us in our lesson plans. So, when looking for a specific category for an objective, it was easy to find. (PST response)

Each of the above PSTs describes an aspect of how they incorporated ChatGPT’s output into their objectives and goals for the lesson, but did not use the output as is.

## Discussion

The results from this study revealed that PSTs engaged in reflective practices when interpreting and using output from ChatGPT for both pedagogy and mathematics content, but were more accurate in their assessments of output related to pedagogy than they were of mathematical output. These results also revealed important limitations in ChatGPT’s usefulness in correctly solving mathematics problems, and for aspects of lesson planning. The emerging literature related to chatbots in educational contexts emphasizes the importance of critique and evaluation of ChatGPT output (Knoth et al., 2024; Trust et al., 2023, van den Berg & du Plessis, 2023; Yilmaz & Yilmaz, 2023), which can be related to the reflective process. In this section, we will first discuss how PSTs reacted to and reflected about ChatGPT’s lesson planning suggestions, then the PSTs’ reflections and reactions related to ChatGPT’s mathematical output, followed by recommendations for the field and limitations.

The Artzt framework, which was familiar to the PSTs in the study, emphasizes the need for critical reflection across all phases of teaching, including planning, monitoring, and evaluating, and Celik et al. (2022) found that AI can be useful for supporting teachers in each of these phases. Knoth et al. (2024) state the importance of users of AI to have “the ability, knowledge, and skills to understand, monitor, effectively interact with, and critically reflect on AI-based technologies” (p. 3), while also noting that it is “essential for teachers to acquire AI-related skills as part of their digital teaching skills” (p. 8). The results of this study support these findings.

The PSTs in this study were able to be reflective and critical about ChatGPT’s output as related to pedagogy. In fact, there was evidence that the PSTs analyzed the pedagogical output through the lens of teaching the overarching mathematical tasks of the lesson, most frequently discussing the sequencing and difficulty levels in the lesson, and the motivational strategies (Artzt, 1999). The PSTs found output such as structured outlines, suggestions for each section of the lesson, prior knowledge, possible mathematical misconceptions, and real-life applications suggested by ChatGPT to be helpful during the preactive stages of the lesson. They found generated homework assignments and assessments to be repetitive, and that teaching approaches were most often teacher-centered. They suggested that ChatGPT’s responses revealed its lack of knowledge of the students and how they learn. This demonstrated the PSTs’ own beliefs about teaching and the importance of knowledge of students in lesson planning, which may have been influenced by the ongoing use of the Artzt framework.

Given the well-documented dificulties that PSTs have in lesson planning (Cevikbas et al., 2024; Lilly et al., 2024; Lim et al., 2018; Sahin-Taskin, 2017), ChatGPT appears to be capable of assisting PSTs in planning in a limited way. Although the PSTs found many issues with ChatGPT’s suggestions for their lessons, analysis also revealed that 20 of the 22 PSTs in this study made changes to their lessons based on ChatGPT’s output. They found suggestions for definitions, objectives, prior knowledge, student misconceptions, and grammar to be useful. They also noted many aspects of the output to not be helpful, again revealing the importance of reflection when interacting with ChatGPT.

When interacting with the mathematical output, results indicated that of the 19 coded solutions for PSTs and the associated ChatGPT output, 15 of the PSTs solved their task correctly while ChatGPT solved it correctly only six times. While it would not necessarily be expected that a PST who solved a problem incorrectly themselves would identify the incorrect response of ChatGPT, it was somewhat surprising that of the 10 PSTs who solved their problem correctly while ChatGPT did not, only one of the PSTs identified ChatGPT’s solution as incorrect. Although it is important to mention that the PSTs were not specifically asked to evaluate the correctness of ChatGPT’s output, they were asked to compare the solutions, and it is not unreasonable to expect that they would be able to identify an incorrect response rather than attribute the “different” solution as simply an alternate approach.

This raises the question of whether the same PSTs would have had the same attribution if the same mistake or misconception had been proposed by one of their students, or even a peer. Aharoni et  al. (2024) may provide some insight given their findings that people found moral guidance generated by ChatGPT to be superior to that of human moral reasoning, cautioning that, “people may uncritically accept potentially harmful moral guidance from AI” (p. 1). These findings may extend to people’s perception of other types of output, including mathematics, although this is weakened by the fact that the PSTs were able to be critical about pedagogy.

The authors have hypothesized that the PSTs’ lack of critical evaluation of mathematics may be related to the non-traditional tasks and their use in an unfamiliar way (i.e., as launch problems for lessons). However, it is more likely that, while they already had a framework for analyzing pedagogy and teaching, they did not have an explicit framework for critical analysis of the mathematics solutions. While it may be impossible to determine, this finding may reveal PSTs’ beliefs about their own content knowledge. That is, they were more trusting of ChatGPT’s mathematical output because they were not certain about their own solutions. Their lack of criticism of ChatGPT could be attributed to their lack of depth of knowledge of the mathematics content, although this would require further research and study to ascertain.

## Recommendations and Conclusions

The results of this study indicate that GAI such as ChatGPT has a place in mathematics teacher preparation as long as PSTs insightfully and critically reflect on the output they receive. Colleges, universities, and teacher preparation programs might consider that GAI can be useful, but not as a self-standing solution. What we have noted in this study is that the accuracy of PSTs’ evaluation of the output may be related to their own beliefs and knowledge, which appeared to be stronger as related to pedagogy than to mathematics, which may be because they had already been using the Artzt (1999) framework to analyze their own work. Their beliefs about the importance of student-centered lessons were revealed through their reflections about the pedagogical output, which is likely related to their use of the Artzt (1999) framework.

As related to the mathematical tasks, PSTs’ beliefs in their own abilities to evaluate these mathematics tasks may have played a role in how they critiqued the output produced by ChatGPT. Their reflections revealed that they did not always identify incorrect mathematical output, which led them to accept incorrect answers and faulty mathematical language produced by ChatGPT as “different approaches”. It seemed, however, when it came to pedagogical approaches their beliefs about and their own understanding of good pedagogy allowed them to be more critical and accurate in their reflections. This may also be attributed to their ongoing use of a framework for recommended pedagogy. Therefore, another framework for evaluating mathematical solutions might be helpful when using GAI.

## Limitations

There were several limitations to this study. We were not able to recruit widely for this study, and it was necessary to examine a convenience sample with a small sample size. Results may be different for other groups of PSTs. We were not able to monitor how the PSTs used ChatGPT during the times when they were encouraged but not required to use the chatbot. Further, the participants worked on a variety of math problems, and the outcomes might have been different had they all worked on the same problem and taught the same lesson. Finally, the fact that these problems were mostly non-routine could have skewed the PSTs’ beliefs in their ability to correctly solve these problems. PSTs might have been better able to recognize errors in ChatGPT’s output if the problems were routine, and might have been more inclined to do so if they were explicitly asked to check for correctness.
