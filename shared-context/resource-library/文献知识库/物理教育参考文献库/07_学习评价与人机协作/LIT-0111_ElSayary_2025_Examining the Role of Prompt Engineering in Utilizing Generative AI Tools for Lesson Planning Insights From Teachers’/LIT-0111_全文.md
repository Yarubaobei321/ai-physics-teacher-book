The purpose of this study is to explore the role of prompt engineering in utilizing GenAI tools like EduAid, Magic School, and
ChatGPT to enhance lesson planning efficiency and content relevance. Additionally, it highlights the need for AI literacy and
continuous professional development to align AI outputs with diverse student needs. A qualitative case study was used to
collect data from 17 high school teachers from private and governmental schools. Document analysis, written reflections, and
focus group talks were used as data sources to get detailed information about how the teachers used and thought about GenAI
tools. The results show that effective prompts helped teachers use GenAI tools better, which makes lesson planning and
material more relevant. Teachers emphasized that AI-generated material needed to be constantly improved to better meet the
needs of all of their students. This study highlights the importance of developing AI literacy through continuous professional
training to effectively enable teachers to integrate AI tools into their teaching practices.
Keywords: AI literacy; generative AI (GenAI) tools; prompt engineering; teacher perceptions
1. Introduction
Education is one of the many industries dramatically
impacted by the quick development of technology [1].
Among these technological advancements, prompt engineering in GenAI tools has emerged as a significant innovation [2, 3]. GenAI tools, including EduAid, Magic School,
and ChatGPT, employ artificial intelligence (AI) to produce
content derived from user input [1, 4]. The effectiveness of
these tools heavily depends on the prompts entered by users,
highlighting the importance of prompt engineering [5].
Prompt engineering involves crafting precise and effective
input to elicit AI systems’most valuable and relevant output
[6]. This study examines the utilization of prompt engineering
by teachers in Generative AI tools for lesson planning, as well
as their experiences and perceptions regarding this practice.
Prompt engineering enhances the quality and relevance
of AI-generated content, establishing it as a crucial skill for
educators [7]. Mastering prompt engineering enables educators to effectively utilize AI tools, thereby optimizing time
management, generating varied educational content, and
customizing lesson plans to address the unique requirements
of their students [8]. For example, teachers used different
student-centered strategies to plan their lessons, such as
the 5Es strategy, problem-based learning, project-based
learning, or engineering design process [9]. Among these,
the 5E model, commonly used by teachers, is widely recognized for its structured approach to lesson planning, focusing on the engage, explore, explain, elaborate, and evaluate
stages [10, 11]. It guides students from the curiosity of asking questions and being engaged in their learning process
to reflection and evaluation.

Research indicates the considerable potential of AI tools
in education, especially in the automation and enhancement
of lesson planning [9–12]. The effectiveness of these tools is
significantly influenced by the quality of the prompts provided by users [13]. Prompt engineering is an essential competency that allows educators to derive the most pertinent
and beneficial content from AI systems [2, 7, 13]. Although
the potential is acknowledged, research specifically examining the role of prompt engineering in educational contexts
remains limited. This study investigates the practical applications of prompt engineering within educational contexts,
contributing to the development of essential AI literacy skills
for the future. Educators must develop AI literacy skills to
effectively integrate AI tools into their teaching practices
[1, 14]. AI literacy encompasses comprehension of AI system functionality, effective interaction with these systems,
and critical evaluation of AI-generated outputs [15]. Prior
research highlights the significance of AI literacy in equipping educators and students for a technology-driven future
[11, 14–16].
Regardless of the growing interest in AI in education,
significant gaps remain in the literature regarding the
practical applications of prompt engineering with GenAI
tools for lesson planning [1]. For example, some studies
have specifically examined how teachers interact with
GenAI tools, the effectiveness of the prompts generated,
and the overall impact on lesson-planning processes [2,
13, 17]. This study addresses these gaps by providing
empirical evidence on using generative AI tools in educational settings and offering recommendations for their
effective integration.
The current literature offers a broad view of AI’s potential in education but often lacks specific insights into the
practical challenges and strategies for effective prompt engineering. For instance, studies by Heston [2] and Nazary et al.
[13] emphasize the importance of prompt quality but do not
explore teachers’ experiences crafting these prompts. Similarly, research by Chiu et al. [7] highlights the advantages
of AI-generated content, yet it does not adequately address
the iterative process educators engage in to refine prompts.
This study seeks to address existing gaps by thoroughly
examining teachers’experiences with GenAI tools, emphasizing the iterative process of prompt engineering and its
influence on lesson planning.
Mastering prompt engineering is essential for AI literacy,
allowing teachers to utilize GenAI tools in lesson planning.
This study enhances existing literature by examining the
development and application of AI literacy skills among
teachers via prompt engineering. This study is essential
due to the increasing incorporation of AI technologies in
education and the imperative to assist teachers in enhancing
their AI and digital literacy skills [18]. In the United Arab
Emirates (UAE), there is a strong emphasis on incorporating
digital tools to enhance educational outcomes, which aligns
with the national agenda of UAE Vision 2021 [19]. The
national agenda is aimed at establishing a first-rate education system using advanced technologies to improve teaching and learning outcomes. Furthermore, the study
supports Sustainable Development Goal 4: Quality Education, which emphasizes the importance of inclusive and
equitable education for all [20, 21].
Reflective practice is a critical component of teacher professional development. Schön’s concepts of reflection-in-action and reflection-on-action provide a robust foundation
for understanding teacher-reflective practices. Reflection-in-action refers to the process by which educators assess their
experiences and implement immediate modifications to
enhance teaching methods and results [22]. This prompt
refl
ection enables educators to adjust their methods in real-time, thereby improving the learning experience as it progresses. Conversely, reflection-on-action necessitates that
educators analyze their experiences postevent, facilitating
comprehensive analysis and fostering long-term enhancements. This type of reflection facilitates a more thorough
evaluation of teaching practices and outcomes, promoting
continuous professional development.
Complementing Schön’s theories, Kolb’s experiential
learning theory emphasizes learning through experience
and reflection. According to Kolb [23], effective learning
occurs through a cyclical process involving concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and
active experimentation. This cyclical process provides a
structured framework for capturing teachers’reflective practices as they interact with AI tools for lesson planning.
Kolb’s model provides a comprehensive understanding of
how teachers learn from their experiences and apply new
knowledge to their practice.
Kolb’s experiential learning model further delineates the
process of experiential learning by incorporating stages of
reflection and conceptualization [23]. This study builds
on this literature by examining how teachers reflect on
their use of AI tools both during the lesson planning process (reflection-in-action) and after completing their lesson
plans (reflection-on-action). By integrating these theories,
the study is aimed at providing a deeper understanding
of teachers’ experiences and insights, thereby informing
the development of practical AI tools for educational
purposes.
This study is aimed at investigating the effectiveness of
teachers in employing prompt engineering when utilizing
GenAI tools for lesson planning. This study evaluates the
effectiveness of prompts created and utilized by teachers,
their experiences with prompt engineering, and their perceptions of various GenAI tools in lesson planning. The following research questions guide the study:
1. How effective are the prompts entered by teachers in
generating lesson plans using generative AI tools?
How effective are the resulting outputs in meeting lesson planning objectives?
2. What are the teachers’experiences with using prompt
engineering in generative AI tools to plan their
lessons?
3. What are the teachers’ perceptions of using prompt
engineering in planning their lessons with different
generative AI tools?

2. Methodology
This study employed a qualitative case study design utilizing
triangulation to explore the effectiveness of prompt engineering in GenAI tools for lesson planning. The qualitative
approach is well-suited to gain in-depth insights into
teachers’experiences and perceptions, particularly in educational research where context and participant perspectives
are crucial [24].
This design is appropriate when the boundaries between
the phenomenon and the context are unclear [25]. This
study examines the effectiveness of prompt engineering
within the context of teachers’ plans utilizing GenAI. The
case study method facilitates an in-depth analysis of this
interaction in its natural context. This study utilized methodological triangulation to improve the validity and reliability of the findings, incorporating multiple data sources to
examine the phenomenon [26]. Triangulation facilitates the
cross-verification of data, yielding a more comprehensive
and balanced understanding of the research problem [27].
2.1. Participants. The participants in this study were 17 high
school teachers purposefully selected from private ( n1=7 ,
41.18%) and governmental ( n2=1 0 , 58.82%) schools. The
primary criterion for selection was the teachers’willingness
to engage in the study and complete the tasks as outlined.
This approach ensured that the participants had a genuine
interest in exploring the potential of AI in their teaching
practice.
The sample consisted of a greater proportion of female
teachers, with 12 (70.59%) females and 5 (29.41%) males.
The teachers instructed a variety of grade levels. Specifically,
one (5.88%) taught Grades 8–9, one (5.88%) taught Grades
8–11, two (11.76%) taught Grades 8–10, and one (5.88%)
taught both Grades 8 and 10. Other teachers indicated sole
grade level teaching: 3 (17.65%) taught Grade 8, 2 (11.76%)
each for Grade 11 and Grade 12, 2 (11.76%) taught Grade
10, and 1 teacher (5.88%) taught Grades 11–12. This indicates
that the surveyed teachers cover a wide range of grade levels.
Science emerged as the most commonly taught subject,
with nine educators (52.94%) instructing in biology, chemistry, physics, environmental science, and general science.
Mathematics accounted for five teachers, representing
29.41% of the total. Each subject, including English, Computer Science, and Geography, was represented by a single
teacher, accounting for 5.88%. This demonstrates a robust
representation of STEM disciplines. Most teachers, 10
(58.82%), indicated 11–15 years of teaching experience. Of
the participants, 7 (41.18%) possessed 6–10 years of teaching
experience, suggesting that all individuals were relatively
experienced in the field of education. The ages of the
teachers exhibited considerable variation, categorized as follows: 3 (17.65%) aged 30–35 years, 7 (41.18%) aged 36–40
years, 6 (35.29%) aged 40–45 years, and 1 (5.88%) aged
46–50 years.
2.2. Instruments
2.2.1. Teachers’ Task (Document Analysis). The task assigned
to the teachers aimed to examine the utilization of GenAI
tools by educators in lesson planning, thereby addressing
the initial research question of the study. The assignment
required developing a 20–30 min lesson plan utilizing backward design principles, focusing on defining end goals first
and then planning in reverse to align objectives, activities,
and assessments [28]. The aim is to document the prompts
submitted by educators to AI tools and the corresponding
outputs produced by these tools.
Teachers were instructed to create a lesson plan utilizing
an AI tool with which they are familiar, including options
such as EduAid.AI, Magic School, and ChatGPT. The document analysis primarily concentrated on the prompts submitted by the teachers and the resultant outputs produced
by the AI tools in the sections outlined in Appendix A. In
this task, teachers anticipated that AI tools would produce
detailed, sequential lesson plans. However, several AI-
generated outputs were overly broad, requiring additional
modifi
cations to fulfill specific classroom requirements.
2.2.2. Teachers’ Written Reflection. The teachers’ written
reflections were an integral part of the study, providing valuable insights into their experiences with GenAI tools for lesson planning, which addresses the second research question
of the study. Using Kolb’s [23] experiential learning model,
the teachers reflected on their activities, observations, challenges, and strategies and considered how their practices
might evolve (see Appendix B for the reflective questions).
2.2.3. Focus Group Discussion. The focus group discussion is
aimed at addressing the third research question of the study.
The objective is to gain a deeper understanding of teachers’
experiences and to collect qualitative insights that cannot
be obtained solely through document analysis and written
reflections (reflection-on-action).
The focus groups were conducted online using the Zoom
conference platform, lasting 45–50 min. Ten teachers participated in the discussion, distributed equally into two groups,
structured around semistructured, open-ended, reflective
questions adapted from Kolb’s experiential learning model.
The questions were sent to two educational experts to determine their face validity and clarity. The experts compared
the questions to the purpose of the study and provided feedback. They commended the questions for their alignment
with the study’s objectives and their ability to elicit detailed
and meaningful responses from the participants (see Appendix C).
2.3. Procedure. A qualitative case study approach was
employed utilizing methodological triangulation, incorporating data collection through document analysis, written
reflections, and focus group discussions.
First, an initial meeting was conducted with the participating teachers to ensure clarity and consistency in task
completion. The meeting outlined the study’s purpose and
presented detailed guidelines. Educators received an overview of the task specifications and the application of backward design in lesson planning. The study discussed the
selected GenAI tools and the documentation procedures
for input and output. After the meeting, teachers received

5. Implications
The implications of this study are that, even with rich
sources of lesson planning in GenAI tools, effective use
entails a combination of continuous professional development, AI literacy, and an iterative model for prompt engineering. The results confirm that effective engineering of
prompts helps teachers make a more direct link between
lesson content and educational objectives. Besides, AI tools
can reduce cognitive overload during lesson planning, and
teachers can then devote their full attention to instruction
and student activity. Teachers learned that AI output, even
when useful, sometimes required refinement and retuning
in an attempt to perfectly tailor it to specific individual
classroom requirements. This underlines not only utilizing
AI tools but also developing competencies for effective
communication with such tools through effective prompt
engineering. Iterative refinement of prompts is an important part of getting high-quality, relevant output, according
to the study.
From a pragmatic perspective, the study promotes
additional training programs for teachers in AI literacy,
particularly in terms of prompt engineering. Teachers with
proper training in developing effective prompts experienced easier incorporation of AI tools in lesson planning.
However, tools must become even more specific and
tailor-made in an effort to cater to the variegated and
ever-changing demands of a classroom environment.
Teachers have expressed a desire for moreflexible AI
capabilities that can respond to changing educational standards, grade levels, and subject-specific requirements.
Additionally, this work highlights the necessity for a balanced model in which AI tools work to amplify, not supplant, teacher expertise. AI can, for example, work to
make parts of lesson planning easier, but teachers will
remain at the center of curating information in a way that
makes it relevant, correct, and sensitive to students’needs.
As AI technology continues to mature, teachers’ expertise
in tailoring AI output for use in their individual educational environments will become ever more critical.

This study has several identified limitations. The study
included teachers who volunteered to participate, which
may introduce self-selection bias. The presence of teachers
with prior interest or proficiency in AI tools may bias the
results, leading to more favorable experiences. The study

7. Recommendations
This study recommends educator training that emphasizes
key areas to improve the effectiveness of generative AI
tools in lesson planning. Comprehensive AI literacy programs must be implemented to ensure that teachers grasp
the fundamentals of AI and demonstrate proficiency in
prompt engineering, especially in the iterative refinement
process. Training should emphasize the role of AI tools
in facilitating personalized learning through customized
strategies that address the varied needs of students. The
training should assist teachers in tailoring AI outputs to
fit particular classroom contexts, thereby ensuring that
AI enhances their professional expertise. Educators should
utilize AI tools to develop formative assessments, including
peer evaluations and exit tickets, to facilitate ongoing student evaluation. The training should prioritize the role of
AI in optimizing lesson planning, thereby minimizing time
allocated to administrative duties, enabling educators to
concentrate on instructional methods, and enhancing student engagement.
8. Conclusion
This study provides critical insights into the significant role
of prompt engineering with GenAI tools in lesson planning,
emphasizing its potential to improve the educational process
substantially. The findings demonstrate that practical and
iterative prompt engineering allows teachers to maximize
the potential of GenAI tools, including EduAid, Magic
School, and ChatGPT, to develop more impactful and pertinent lesson content. Effective prompt engineering facilitates
the development of customized lesson plans that cater to
varied student needs and enhance efficiency in the planning
process, as evidenced by the research of Chiu et al. [7] and
Nazary et al. [13].
The findings indicate that although GenAI tools offer
teachers organized and efficient solutions, manual refinement of AI outputs is frequently required to align with particular classroom needs. This highlights the significance of
AI literacy in education, especially the iterative refinement
process essential for enhancing AI-generated content [2, 7].
Teachers reported favorable experiences with these tools,
indicating that prompt engineering enhanced the quality
and relevance of lesson outputs [1]. Nonetheless, specific
challenges, including the AI’s restricted capacity to adequately address the diverse abilities of students, were also
observed [11].
This study emphasizes the necessity of ongoing professional development in AI literacy, corresponding with the
essential requirement for educators to effectively incorporate AI tools into their instructional methods [1, 14].
The evolution of AI necessitates that educators maintain
proficiency in developing prompts and refining AI outputs
to align with educational standards. The World Bank [18]
emphasized that fostering AI literacy skills is essential for
educators to promote inclusive and equitable learning
environments.
This study confirms that prompt engineering is essential
for educators to optimize using GenAI tools. Although these
tools provide significant advantages in time efficiency and
content creation, the expertise of teachers is critical for refining AI outputs to align with curriculum standards and
address specific student needs. As GenAI shapes the future
of education, it is necessary to equip educators with prompt
engineering skills and AI literacy to promote high-quality,
personalized, and inclusive education [13, 21].
